The CV used to open with a job title and a list of projects in the order I
finished them. That was honest, and it was the wrong first impression: the
newest thing a reader met was three years old, and the work I most want to be
judged on sat halfway down a scroll.

Two weeks of applications made the cost visible. Recruiters quoted the solar
valuation years back at me and skimmed the fleet. So the page now leads with
what I actually do. The hero reads Site Reliability and Platform Engineer; the
German page reads Site-Reliability- und Plattform-Ingenieur. The subtitle names
the four things a reader can test rather than take on trust: NixOS fleets,
alerting and rollback, Go services, operational data.

Below that, order is data instead of chronology. `projects.json` carries a
weight per project and the archive sorts by it: nixconfig at 9, the KEV lab
tools at 8, the 2020 product work at 2, the stream work at 1. Nothing is
hidden, and every case study is still one click down. The order just stops
pretending that the most recent date is the most relevant evidence.

The bio was rewritten last, and it is the only sentence I would keep if the
page had room for one: I build the systems and the evidence that they are still
working. Everything above it is an illustration of that sentence.

Two things did not change. The site still ships no webfonts, no cookies and no
third-party scripts: nineteen static routes, five CV PDFs and an Open Graph
card, all rendered from the same JSON the page itself reads. And the content is
the same work. This is a reordering of evidence, not a new portfolio. If the
fleet is not what a role needs, a better hero cannot rescue the application; it
can only keep the reader from missing the part that matters.
