A token stream is a one-dimensional thing, and so is a file of image bytes. The
only question the gap asks is what order to read them in.

Raster order answers by rows: byte one is the top left pixel, byte two the one to
its right, and a line of text becomes a line of the picture. Then a byte and its
neighbour in the stream are neighbours on the canvas only until the row ends, and
the last byte of a row sits an edge away from a byte it contributes nothing to.

A Hilbert curve answers differently. It visits every cell of the square exactly
once, and consecutive cells are always touching, so consecutive bytes are always
touching pixels. The picture keeps the stream's locality: a repeated word becomes
a patch, a change early in the text moves a region near the start of the curve,
and a range of bytes occupies a region of the picture with a bounding box that can
be computed rather than guessed.

That last sentence is the whole claim. A span of bytes on the curve is a set of
pixels whose bounds are arithmetic: split the span into the aligned blocks the
curve already knows about, take each block's square, and the hull of those squares
is exact. No approximation, no scan, no model. The same arithmetic answers where
the tokens a person edited landed in the image, and it answers whether a variation
stayed inside the range it declared.

Nothing here is a diffusion model, and nothing here is trained. The bytes are the
tokenizer's own output, read in an order that preserves what the text was doing.
Variations are typed range operations over those bytes: roll a span, splice one
span into another, reverse it, gain it, mask it. Each one declares the range it is
allowed to touch, and the report either shows that every changed byte lies inside
a declared range or fails.

So the image is not a rendering of a prompt's meaning. It is the prompt's bytes,
at the positions the curve gives them, and a set of declared changes you can point
at.
